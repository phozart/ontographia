Mental Models & Sensemaking Studio
A General-Purpose Reasoning & Thinking Environment (with Integrated Experience Lens)

You are building a Mental Models & Sensemaking Studio.

This studio is a general-purpose thinking environment that helps users:

reason about complex situations

surface assumptions and narratives

compare perspectives

explore uncertainty

understand consequences and trade-offs

reflect on experience and perception when relevant

It is not:

a management tool

a training platform

a methodology enforcer

a CX, PM, BA, or ITSM system

a measurement or KPI tool

Its purpose is to externalise thinking, not to standardise it.

1. Core Design Principles (Non-Negotiable)

Thinking before structure
Users start with vague situations and partial thoughts.
The system must tolerate incompleteness, contradiction, and evolution.

Lenses, not workflows
The studio offers ways of thinking, not steps to follow.

Experience is a perspective, not a discipline
CX is integrated as an Experience Lens, never as a separate module.

Guidance is subtle and optional
Prompts suggest reflection; they never enforce correctness.

Broad applicability
The studio must work for:

business analysis

strategy

product/service reasoning

organisational diagnosis

decision preparation

personal reflection

2. Shared Sensemaking Model (Required)

All lenses, diagrams, and exercises use the same underlying model.

2.1 Core Entities
Situation

A concrete context or question being explored.

title

description

scope (optional)

tags

All thinking attaches to a Situation.

Thought

A basic unit of thinking.

text

type: idea | observation | assumption | belief | concern | option | question | experience-step

confidence (optional)

source (self | other | implicit)

Thoughts are intentionally generic and flexible.

Frame

A way of interpreting or explaining the situation.

label (plain language)

description

frameType: mental_model | narrative | heuristic | bias_hypothesis | perspective

confidence (optional)

Examples:

“Efficiency reduces risk”

“Customers value predictability over speed”

Alternative

A competing frame or interpretation.

description

what it changes

what it worsens (explicit trade-off)

Implication

A consequence if a frame or thought holds.

description

impact: low | medium | high

reversibility: easy | medium | hard (optional)

Boundary

Where a frame, assumption, or analogy stops applying.

description

importance

Evidence (optional)

Anchors thinking to reality.

note or link

supports or challenges a Thought or Frame

strength: low | medium | high

2.2 Relationships

Situation HAS Thought

Frame APPLIES_TO Situation

Frame EXPLAINS Thought

Alternative CHALLENGES Frame or Thought

Frame or Alternative IMPLIES Implication

Frame HAS Boundary

Evidence SUPPORTS / REFUTES Thought or Frame

Thought RELATES_TO Thought (free-form)

3. Interaction Model
3.1 Thinking Canvas (Primary UI)

The primary interface is a canvas where users can:

add thoughts freely

connect, cluster, and annotate ideas

switch lenses and views without losing content

No rigid forms. No mandatory completion.

3.2 Multiple Views on the Same Thinking

The same underlying data must be viewable as:

free-form thought map (mind-map style)

clustered views

matrices (user-defined axes)

comparison tables

scenario sketches

narrative / linear views

Views never create new data; they only project it.

4. Built-In Lenses (Presets)

Lenses are thinking modes defined by:

prompts

highlighted entity types

default views

Users can switch lenses at any time.

4.1 Mental Models & Assumptions Lens

Focus:

implicit assumptions

dominant narratives

mental shortcuts

Prompts:

“What are we assuming is fixed?”

“What feels obvious but isn’t proven?”

“What belief is shaping these decisions?”

4.2 Counterfactual / Opposite Lens

Focus:

challenging dominant frames

Prompts:

“If the opposite were true, what changes?”

“What are we optimising for without noticing?”

“What would a critic say?”

4.3 Trade-offs & Tensions Lens

Focus:

unavoidable dilemmas

Prompts:

“What improves if this holds?”

“What necessarily worsens?”

“What tension cannot be resolved?”

4.4 Scenario & Futures Lens (Lightweight)

Focus:

holding multiple plausible futures

Tools:

2×2 uncertainty matrix

scenario cards

implication mapping

Prompts:

“What if this assumption fails?”

“What changes under a different future?”

Scenarios are sketches, not forecasts.

4.5 Pattern & Analogy Lens

Focus:

learning transfer

“this resembles…”

Prompts:

“Where have we seen this before?”

“What pattern does this resemble?”

“Where does the analogy break?”

5. Integrated Experience (CX) Lens
5.1 Positioning

The Experience Lens is a temporary perspective that shifts thinking from:

internal logic → external lived experience

It does not introduce new artefacts or data structures.

5.2 When the System Suggests the Experience Lens

The system should suggest (never force) activating the Experience Lens when:

Situation text contains experiential language
(e.g. confusing, frustrating, trust, effort, unclear, complaints)

Implications reference perception or emotion
(e.g. confidence, reputation, stress, satisfaction)

Multiple internal frames exist with no external perspective

Repeated adjustments exist without perceived improvement

Suggestion text example:

“This situation appears to involve lived experience or perception.
You might want to explore it using an Experience Lens.”

5.3 Experience Lens Prompts

When active, show prompts such as:

“How does this feel from the outside?”

“What effort does this create?”

“Where might trust be gained or lost?”

“What would a first-time user experience here?”

“Which moment shapes the lasting impression?”

These prompts generate:

Thoughts (type = experience-step)

Frames (experience-based narratives)

Implications (high-impact moments)

5.4 Experience-Oriented Views (Derived)

When the Experience Lens is active, default views may include:

Experience Timeline

A linear view of experience-related Thoughts over time.

Moment-of-Truth Highlight

Highlights Implications with high experiential impact.

Intent vs Experience Contrast

Side-by-side comparison:

Internal Frame (“We designed it to be efficient”)

Experience Frame (“It feels rushed and opaque”)

No journeys, personas, or metrics are stored as permanent objects.

6. Guidance System (Across All Lenses)

Guidance must be:

prompt-based

dismissible

non-judgmental

Examples:

“Only one frame detected. Consider adding an alternative.”

“No boundaries captured yet — where might this break?”

“This frame has strong implications but low evidence.”

No scoring. No correctness checks.

7. Extensibility

The system must allow:

adding new lenses without schema changes

custom prompts

custom diagram presets

saving and reusing exercises

8. Non-Functional Requirements

Time-to-first-insight < 5 minutes

Partial input allowed

Undo / redo always available

Fast canvas interaction

No mandatory fields

9. Success Criteria

The studio is successful if users say:

“This helped me see it differently.”

“This made assumptions visible.”

“This clarified why we’re stuck.”

It fails if:

it feels like training

it feels like documentation

users ask “what do I need to fill in?”

10. Internal Positioning Statement

“This studio helps people think better about complex situations by making assumptions, perspectives, experience, and consequences visible — without telling them what to think.”



SYSTEM PROMPT
Academic Learning Studio
A Meta-Learning & Study Sensemaking Environment

You are building an Academic Learning Studio.

This studio supports how people learn, not what they store.
It helps users think intentionally about how to study, how to understand, and how to adapt their learning approach over time.

It is not:

a learning management system (LMS)

a note-taking or knowledge base tool

a flashcard or memorisation app

a grading or assessment system

a productivity or gamification tool

Its purpose is to support academic thinking, learning strategy, and reflection.

1. Core Design Intent (Non-Negotiable)

Learning is a cognitive process, not a content repository
The system focuses on learning modes, understanding, and reflection, not storing material.

Concrete learning sessions matter
Learning should be grounded in real, time-boxed study sessions, not abstract intentions.

Guidance without prescription
The system guides learning choices through prompts and reflections, never through enforced workflows.

Academic, not performative
No scores, streaks, grades, KPIs, or productivity pressure.

Reflection is first-class
Learning improves through reflection on how one studied, not just what was studied.

2. Conceptual Scope

The Academic Learning Studio exists to answer questions such as:

“How should I approach learning this subject?”

“Am I memorising when I should be understanding?”

“Why does this topic not click yet?”

“How should I adapt my learning strategy?”

It supports:

university study

self-directed academic learning

preparation for exams, essays, and discussions

conceptual, analytical, and critical learning

3. Core Learning Model (Required)

The studio must be built on a small, explicit set of learning primitives.

3.1 Learning Situation

A bounded learning context.

Examples:

“Studying political philosophy for exam”

“Understanding system dynamics chapter 4”

“Preparing academic essay on freedom of speech”

Fields:

title

description

subject / course (optional)

learning objective (free text)

constraints (time, deadline, difficulty; optional)

Learning Situations frame why learning is happening.

3.2 Learning Mode (Critical Concept)

Represents how the learner intends to think.

Learning modes are cognitive intentions, not activities.

Supported modes (extendable):

Exploration (first exposure)

Conceptual understanding

Analytical comparison

Critical evaluation

Memorisation

Synthesis (writing, explaining, integrating)

Application / transfer

Each Learning Session must reference a Learning Mode.

3.3 Learning Session

A concrete, time-boxed act of learning.

Fields:

start time

end time (or duration)

learning mode

focus question (“What am I trying to understand?”)

material used (optional reference)

interruptions or friction notes (optional)

The system should support:

manual start/stop

optional timer (non-gamified, neutral)

Timers exist to ground learning in reality, not to optimise output.

3.4 Understanding Signal

A qualitative reflection on level of understanding.

Examples:

“I can explain this in my own words”

“I recognise the structure but not the details”

“I’m memorising without understanding”

“I can apply this to a new example”

“I feel confused about the core idea”

These are signals, not scores.

3.5 Reflection & Adjustment

A short reflection captured after a learning session.

Core prompts:

What clicked?

What confused me?

Was the chosen learning mode appropriate?

What should I change next time?

Reflections should be:

short

optional but encouraged

easy to capture

4. User Flow (High-Level, Non-Linear)
Typical learning loop (not enforced):

Define or select a Learning Situation

Start a Learning Session

Choose a Learning Mode and focus question

Study (with optional timer)

Capture Understanding Signal

Reflect briefly

Adjust next session approach

The user may enter at any step.

5. Guidance System (Key Value)

Guidance must be:

prompt-based

contextual

dismissible

non-judgmental

5.1 Before a Session

Prompts such as:

“What kind of understanding are you aiming for in this session?”

“Is this exploration, understanding, or memorisation?”

“What question do you want to answer?”

5.2 During a Session (Optional, Minimal)

Very light prompts:

“Still aligned with your learning intention?”

“Is this mode still serving your goal?”

No interruptions by default.

5.3 After a Session

Reflection prompts:

“What actually happened?”

“Did this mode help?”

“What would you do differently next time?”

5.4 Pattern-Based Suggestions (Optional)

The system may suggest reflections such as:

“Several sessions focus on reading; synthesis might help now.”

“Confusion persists across sessions — try a different learning mode.”

“You often memorise before understanding; is that intentional?”

Suggestions are never mandatory.

6. Views & Representations

The studio must offer multiple views on the same learning data:

6.1 Learning Timeline

Sessions over time

Learning modes used

Understanding signals

6.2 Learning Mode Distribution

Qualitative overview of how learning time is spent

No percentages or targets enforced

6.3 Reflection View

Aggregated reflections

Patterns in confusion, insight, or strategy shifts

7. Integration with Mental Models & Sensemaking Studio

The Academic Learning Studio must integrate conceptually with the Mental Models & Sensemaking Studio.

Examples:

A Learning Situation can spawn a Sensemaking Situation

Users can reflect on:

“What mental model does this theory use?”

“What assumptions does this discipline make?”

Critical or philosophical questions can be handed off smoothly

No duplication of content; integration is conceptual.

8. What the System Must Explicitly Avoid

Do NOT include:

grades or marks

progress percentages

learning streaks

productivity scores

task lists or to-dos

content repositories

If users attempt to add these, the system should gently remind:

“This studio focuses on learning process, not performance measurement.”

9. Non-Functional Requirements

Time to start a session: < 30 seconds

Time to reflect: < 2 minutes

Partial input allowed everywhere

Undo / edit always possible

Calm, academic UX (no pressure cues)

10. Success Criteria

The Academic Learning Studio is successful if users say:

“I’m more aware of how I’m studying.”

“I know when to change my learning approach.”

“I understand subjects more deeply, not just faster.”

It fails if:

it feels like a productivity tracker

it feels like an LMS

users ask “how do I complete this?”

11. Internal Positioning Statement

“This studio supports academic learning by making learning intentions, cognitive modes, and understanding visible — without measuring or judging performance.”

SYSTEM PROMPT
Philosophical & Critical Thinking Studio
A Disciplined Reasoning & Reflection Environment

You are building a Philosophical & Critical Thinking Studio.

This studio supports careful reasoning, conceptual clarity, and critical examination of ideas.
It helps users think more precisely, question assumptions, analyse arguments, and explore meaning.

It is not:

a philosophy encyclopedia

a history-of-philosophy reference

a debate or persuasion tool

a note-taking or citation system

an assessment or grading environment

Its purpose is to support thinking well, not being right.

1. Core Design Intent (Non-Negotiable)

Philosophy as disciplined thinking, not doctrine
The studio focuses on how to think, not what to believe.

Clarity before conclusion
The system prioritises:

clear concepts

explicit assumptions

well-formed arguments
over opinions or answers.

Grounded abstraction
Even abstract questions must be anchored in:

concrete claims

implications

limits

Non-adversarial reasoning
This is not about winning arguments, but about understanding them.

Slow thinking encouraged
The studio must feel calm, reflective, and non-urgent.

2. Scope & Use Cases

The Philosophical & Critical Thinking Studio supports:

examining philosophical questions

clarifying ambiguous concepts

analysing arguments and counterarguments

exploring ethical and normative dilemmas

improving critical reasoning skills

reflecting on meaning, values, and assumptions

It is suitable for:

academic philosophy

social and political reasoning

ethical reflection in professional contexts

deep conceptual thinking across disciplines

3. Shared Reasoning Model (Reuse Required)

The studio must reuse the shared Sensemaking Model used across the platform.

3.1 Core Entities (Philosophy-specific interpretation)
Philosophical Question

A question that requires reasoning, not factual lookup.

Examples:

“What is freedom?”

“Is efficiency a moral value?”

“When is authority legitimate?”

Fields:

question text

why this matters (optional)

scope or context (optional)

Concept

A term that requires clarification.

Fields:

concept name

working definition

alternative definitions

ambiguity notes

confidence in definition (optional)

Concepts must be treated as open to revision.

Claim

A statement asserted as true or defended.

Fields:

statement

claim type: descriptive | normative | evaluative

confidence (optional)

source (self, author, tradition, implicit)

Argument

A structured relationship between claims.

Fields:

premises (one or more Claims)

conclusion (Claim)

reasoning type (deductive, inductive, abductive; optional)

Arguments are examined, not accepted or rejected by default.

Counterargument

A challenge to an argument.

Fields:

target argument or claim

challenge type: validity | soundness | assumption | scope

explanation

Implication

What follows if a claim or argument is accepted.

Fields:

implication description

type: ethical | social | practical | conceptual

impact (low / medium / high)

Boundary / Limit

Where a concept, claim, or argument stops applying.

Fields:

boundary description

why this matters

Perspective (Optional but important)

A philosophical stance or viewpoint.

Examples:

utilitarian

deontological

liberal

communitarian

existential

Fields:

perspective label

core commitments

what it prioritises

what it downplays

4. Thinking Tools & Exercises (Lens-Based)

The studio must provide thinking exercises, not mandatory workflows.

Each exercise is:

optional

prompt-driven

grounded in the shared model

4.1 Concept Clarification Lens

Purpose:

reduce ambiguity

distinguish similar ideas

Prompts:

“What do we mean by this term?”

“What does this definition include or exclude?”

“How might others define this differently?”

Views:

definition comparison

ambiguity map

4.2 Argument Examination Lens

Purpose:

analyse structure and strength of reasoning

Prompts:

“What is being claimed?”

“What must be true for this to hold?”

“Does the conclusion follow?”

Views:

argument map (premises → conclusion)

assumption highlighting

4.3 Assumption & Presupposition Lens

Purpose:

surface hidden foundations

Prompts:

“What is taken for granted here?”

“What would someone disagreeing reject first?”

“Is this assumption empirical or normative?”

4.4 Counter-Perspective Lens

Purpose:

test robustness through opposition

Prompts:

“What would a critic say?”

“What is the strongest opposing view?”

“What survives this challenge?”

This lens must encourage charitable interpretation, not straw-manning.

4.5 Ethical Reasoning Lens

Purpose:

explore value conflicts and moral implications

Prompts:

“Who is affected?”

“What values are in tension?”

“What trade-off is unavoidable?”

Views:

value tension map

implication comparison

4.6 Reductio & Limit Testing Lens (Gentle)

Purpose:

test coherence

Prompts:

“What happens if this principle is applied universally?”

“At what point does this break down?”

Use carefully; no forced logic puzzles.

5. Interaction Model
5.1 Reasoning Canvas

Primary interface:

nodes: concepts, claims, arguments

edges: supports, challenges, implies

clustering and annotation supported

Free-form, calm, deliberate.

5.2 Multiple Representations

The same reasoning can be viewed as:

argument trees

concept maps

implication lists

narrative explanations

No single “correct” view.

6. Guidance System (Critical)

Guidance must be:

reflective

non-authoritative

optional

Examples:

“This concept is used in multiple senses — consider clarifying.”

“This argument relies on an unstated assumption.”

“You have strong implications but unclear premises.”

Never:

“This is wrong”

“This is a fallacy” (unless user explicitly labels it so)

7. Integration with Other Studios
With Academic Learning Studio

Philosophical questions can emerge from learning situations

Learning sessions can transition into philosophical examination

Reflection on how one reasons while studying philosophy

With Mental Models Studio

Philosophical frames enrich mental model exploration

Bias awareness connects naturally to epistemology

With Negotiation & Persuasion Studio

Clarifies normative disagreements vs interest conflicts

Helps distinguish persuasion from ethical disagreement

8. What the System Must Explicitly Avoid

Do NOT include:

correctness scoring

argument ranking

automated fallacy detection

debate timers

forced formal logic notation

The system supports thinking, not adjudication.

9. Non-Functional Requirements

Calm, uncluttered UI

Encourages slower interaction

Partial reasoning allowed

Easy revision of definitions and claims

No pressure cues or gamification

10. Success Criteria

The Philosophical & Critical Thinking Studio is successful if users say:

“I think more clearly now.”

“I understand why this is difficult.”

“I can articulate my position better.”

“I see where disagreement really lies.”

It fails if:

it feels like a test

it feels like debate prep

users search for “the right answer”

11. Internal Positioning Statement

“This studio supports philosophical and critical thinking by making concepts, assumptions, arguments, and implications visible — without judging or resolving them.”
